## Highlights

- Increasing the blend wall to E15 will **not affect NOx emissions**
- **Particulate emissions will be reduced** with higher ethanol
- Larger fraction of sub-23 nm particles with higher ethanol blending
- Strong acetaldehyde increases with ethanol, but mixed monoaromatic hydrocarbon emissions decrease
- **Greater life cycle GHG benefits with E15 than E10**

## Abstract

This study assessed the emissions from a fleet of 20 Tier 3 light-duty vehicles when operated with E10 and E15 fuels. The E10 fuel met the specifications to be classified as a California Reformulated Gasoline, while the E15 fuel was splash blended with denature ethanol from the E10 fuel. The test vehicles were representative of the current California and US fleet and were selected from a representative mix of technology groups and manufacturers. Emissions testing was performed over triplicate FTP cycles.

This study is part of a larger effort to evaluate the potential of expanding California's automotive gasoline blend wall to E15. Testing was conducted over the Federal Test Procedure (FTP) cycle for a fleet of 20 light-duty vehicles of different technologies and emission standards. This is the **largest study to date in the US** focusing on the effects of ethanol fuels on tailpipe emissions from current technology vehicles.

Key question addressed: whether introducing E15 in California will cause NOx tailpipe increases. Result: **no NOx increase**; particulate and CO2 emissions reduced with E15.

## Key Findings

- **No NOx penalty**: E15 does not increase NOx tailpipe emissions vs E10
- **Lower PM/PN**: Particulate matter and particle number emissions reduced with E15
- **Smaller particles**: Higher fraction of sub-23 nm particles (less health concern)
- **Lower CO2**: Reduced tailpipe CO2 emissions
- **Better lifecycle GHG**: Life cycle GHG savings with E15 vs E10
- **Trade-off**: Acetaldehyde increases (carbonyl pollutant); BTEX (benzene, toluene, etc.) decrease
- **Ozone potential**: Slightly lower ozone-forming potential for high ethanol blends

## Context

The "ethanol blend wall" refers to the practical limit of how much ethanol can be blended into gasoline before causing vehicle compatibility issues. E10 (10% ethanol) is the US standard; E15 (15% ethanol) is approved for vehicles 2001 and newer but faces regulatory and market barriers in California due to air quality concerns.

California has some of the strictest environmental regulations in the US, with non-attainment areas (South Coast Air Basin, San Joaquin Valley) for PM2.5 and ozone. Prior to adopting any new fuel in California, an in-depth evaluation of environmental impacts is required.

Ethanol's benefits: high-octane rating enables lower GHG emissions and better fuel economy. California's Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) creates economic incentive for higher ethanol blends due to ethanol's lower carbon intensity.

## Methods

- **Vehicles**: 20 Tier 3 light-duty vehicles, representative mix of technology groups and manufacturers
- **Fuels**: Summer-grade CaRFG (E10) from 4 refineries + splash-blended E15 (denature ethanol added to E10)
- **Cycle**: Federal Test Procedure (FTP) — triplicate runs
- **Fleet composition**: Diverse mix of vehicle technologies and emission standards reflecting current California/US fleet
